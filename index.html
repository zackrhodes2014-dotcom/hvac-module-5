🧊 Start Here: Gases

In Module 4 you learned how heat moves and how to measure it. Now we'll work with the stuff that actually carries heat through a system: refrigerant gas. You'll learn why techs add 14.7 to gauge readings and how pressure reveals a refrigerant's temperature.

1. Volume

How much room a gas takes up

Volume is the amount of space something occupies. It's measured in cubic feet (ft³) or cubic inches in US units, and cubic meters or liters in SI. A gas doesn't have a volume of its own; it expands to fill whatever holds it. Give the same pound of vapor more room and it spreads thinner; give it less and it crowds together. Volume, pressure, and temperature are tied together, and the gas laws describe how.

2. Pressure: Absolute vs. Gauge

Where does zero start?

Pressure is force spread over an area, measured in pounds per square inch (psi). The air around us presses down at about 14.7 psi at sea level.

  • Gauge pressure (psig) treats atmospheric pressure as zero. Your manifold gauges read psig. An open gauge reads 0 psig.
  • Absolute pressure (psia) starts at a perfect vacuum, true zero.
  • psia = psig + 14.7 (at sea level)

Below atmospheric pressure, gauges show vacuum in inches of mercury (inHg). Deep vacuum is measured in microns (1 micron = 1/1,000 mm of mercury). Perfect vacuum is 0 microns, and atmospheric pressure is about 760,000 microns.

Dual-scale gauge

psia = psig + 14.7 (sea level). Vacuum: inHg ≈ (14.7 − psia) × 2.036; microns ≈ psia × 51,715.

3. The Four Gas Laws

Squeeze it, heat it, predict it

Each law holds one thing constant and links the other two. Every pressure and temperature must be absolute: psia, and degrees Rankine (°R = °F + 460).

  1. Boyle's law (constant temperature): P₁V₁ = P₂V₂. Halve the volume and the pressure doubles. Example: 1 ft³ of air at 14.7 psia squeezed to 0.25 ft³ reaches 58.8 psia.
  2. Charles' law (constant pressure): V₁/T₁ = V₂/T₂. Gas expands as it warms.
  3. Gay-Lussac's law (constant volume): P₁/T₁ = P₂/T₂. Heat a sealed container and the pressure goes up.
  4. Combined gas law: (P₁V₁)/T₁ = (P₂V₂)/T₂, for when all three change.
🧮 Worked example: a heated nitrogen cylinder

A sealed nitrogen cylinder reads 2,000 psig at 70°F and is left in a truck that heats up to 120°F. The volume is fixed, so we use Gay-Lussac's law.

  • P₁ = 2,000 + 14.7 = 2,014.7 psia
  • T₁ = 70 + 460 = 530°R; T₂ = 120 + 460 = 580°R
  • P₂ = 2,014.7 × (580 ÷ 530) ≈ 2,204.8 psia
  • Back to gauge: 2,204.8 − 14.7 ≈ 2,190 psig

That's a rise of about 190 psi.

⏸️ Pause and Predict: What answer do you get if you plug 2,000 psig, 70°F, and 120°F straight into P₂ = P₁ × (T₂ ÷ T₁)? Is it right?

Your answer:
2,000 × (120 ÷ 70) ≈ 3,429 psig, which is wildly wrong. See the common error below.
⚠️ Common error: Plugging °F and psig straight into the formula. 2,000 × (120 ÷ 70) ≈ 3,429 psig, which is wildly wrong. Fahrenheit's zero isn't true zero, so the ratio is meaningless. Always convert to Rankine and psia first.

Note: This works for a cylinder holding only gas, like nitrogen. A refrigerant cylinder holding liquid and vapor follows its pressure-temperature (PT) chart instead (see Section 4).

4. Saturated Vapor and Superheat

When pressure tells you temperature

When liquid and vapor refrigerant are together, as in a boiling evaporator or a cylinder that's partly liquid, the refrigerant is saturated. At saturation, each pressure matches exactly one temperature, the saturation temperature. A PT chart lists these pairs for each refrigerant. So a gauge reading tells you the boiling temperature inside a coil.

  • Superheat = actual vapor temperature − saturation temperature. Once all the liquid boils off, more heat raises the vapor's temperature above saturation. Example: the suction line reads 50°F, and the PT chart gives 40°F saturation. Superheat = 10°F.
  • Subcooling works the same way on the liquid side: saturation temperature − actual liquid temperature.

Superheat confirms that only vapor reaches the compressor. Liquid can damage it.

5. Dalton's Law and Pulling a Vacuum

Every gas pulls its own weight

Dalton's law: In a mix of gases, the total pressure equals the sum of each gas's partial pressure. Each gas acts as if it were alone in the space.

Before charging, a system holds air (nitrogen and oxygen) and water vapor. Your micron gauge reads the total of those partial pressures. A vacuum pump removes them, and as pressure drops, moisture boils at lower temperatures. At about 70°F, water boils near 19,000 microns. At a few hundred microns, its boiling point is well below freezing.

Why it matters on the job:

  • When the pressure rises and levels off after you isolate the pump, moisture is still boiling off. Keep pulling.
  • When the pressure keeps rising steadily, air is leaking in. Find the leak.
  • Triple evacuation breaks the vacuum with dry nitrogen, which dilutes the water vapor's partial pressure so that more moisture leaves with each pull.

6. The Refrigeration Cycle

Four steps, one loop

  1. Compression: The compressor squeezes low-pressure vapor into hot, high-pressure vapor.
  2. Condensation: In the condenser, the vapor gives up heat to outdoor air and turns to liquid. That's the hot air off the coil from Module 4.
  3. Expansion: The metering device (TXV, piston, or capillary tube) drops the pressure sharply, and some liquid flashes to vapor as it cools.
  4. Evaporation: In the evaporator, cold low-pressure refrigerant boils by absorbing heat from indoor air, then returns to the compressor.

Heat is absorbed indoors through latent heat at low pressure and rejected outdoors at high pressure.
